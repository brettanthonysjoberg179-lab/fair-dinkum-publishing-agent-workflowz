*Niche identification, keyword analysis, competitor mapping, and the scoring algorithm that separates winners from distractions.*

## The research pipeline

Market research isn't a single step — it's a three-phase process that transforms a raw niche idea into a quantified opportunity.

1. **Discovery:** Find related topics, sub-niches, and adjacent markets.
2. **Analysis:** Map competitors, estimate demand, and identify content gaps.
3. **Scoring:** Weight the factors that predict success and produce a clear go/no-go signal.

The Market Researcher handles phases 1-2. The Opportunity Scorer handles phase 3.

## Phase 1: Discovery

The Market Researcher starts with a seed keyword from the Product Director's brief. It then expands outward using three techniques:

### Keyword expansion

```
seed: "native plant gardening australia"
expanded: ["australian native plants", "indigenous gardening",
           "waterwise natives", "bush tucker garden",
           "drought tolerant native garden", "local provenance plants"]
```

The researcher uses thesaurus relationships, search suggestion patterns, and category taxonomies to build the expansion list. In mock mode, it uses a curated niche ontology. In live mode, it queries keyword research APIs.

### Adjacent niche discovery

Once the core keywords are identified, the researcher looks sideways. Who else is interested in native plant gardening, and what else do they buy?

```
adjacent: ["sustainable landscaping", "wildlife gardening",
           "organic vegetable growing", "permaculture design",
           "heritage fruit trees", "no-dig gardening"]
```

This adjacent analysis reveals cross-selling opportunities and audience overlap that pure keyword research misses.

### Seasonal pattern detection

Some niches have strong seasonal demand. Gardening peaks in spring and autumn. Tax books peak in June-July. The researcher identifies these patterns so the launch strategist can time publication.

## Phase 2: Analysis

With the niche landscape mapped, the researcher gathers data on each competitor and demand signal.

### Competitor matrix

For each identified competitor, the researcher records:

```python
@dataclass
class Competitor:
    title: str
    author: str
    platform: str  # Amazon, Gumroad, Kobo, etc.
    price: float
    rating: float
    review_count: int
    publication_date: date
    page_count: int
    content_quality: str  # assessed from reviews and previews
    sales_estimate: int   # derived from review velocity
    strengths: list[str]
    weaknesses: list[str]
```

The competitor matrix isn't just a list — it's a map. The researcher identifies clusters (many competitors at similar price points) and gaps (underserved topics or price ranges).

### Demand signals

Demand is estimated from three sources:

1. **Search volume:** How many people search for these keywords monthly?
2. **Social discussion:** What's the volume of conversation on Reddit, forums, and social platforms?
3. **Competitor sales velocity:** How fast are existing books accumulating reviews?

Each signal is normalised to a 0-100 scale and combined into a composite demand score.

### Content gap analysis

The researcher identifies what competitors are missing:

```
gaps:
  - topic: "native plants for rental gardens"
    demand: medium
    competition: none
    rationale: "Tenants can't plant in ground; pot-based native gardens are unserved"
  
  - topic: "indigenous fire-resistant landscaping"
    demand: growing
    competition: low
    rationale: "Bushfire resilience is top-of-mind for Australian homeowners"
```

These gaps feed directly into the Positioning Strategist, who crafts the unique angle.

## Phase 3: Scoring

The Opportunity Scorer takes the market research and produces a single number: the opportunity score. But that number is built from weighted components.

### Scoring factors

| Factor | Weight | Description |
|--------|--------|-------------|
| Demand | 25% | How many people want this? |
| Competition | 20% | How hard is it to stand out? |
| Monetisation | 20% | Will people pay, and how much? |
| Author fit | 15% | Does the author have credibility? |
| Timing | 10% | Is this niche rising or falling? |
| Production feasibility | 10% | Can we produce this to a high standard? |

### The scoring formula

```python
def calculate_opportunity_score(research: MarketReport, author: AuthorProfile) -> OpportunityScore:
    demand_score = normalise(research.demand_signals, max=100)
    competition_score = 100 - normalise(research.competitor_density, max=100)  # inverted
    monetisation_score = normalise(research.price_tiers, max=100)
    author_fit_score = calculate_author_fit(research.topic, author.expertise)
    timing_score = normalise(research.trend_velocity, max=100)
    feasibility_score = estimate_feasibility(research.topic, research.required_depth)
    
    return OpportunityScore(
        total=weighted_average([
            (demand_score, 0.25),
            (competition_score, 0.20),
            (monetisation_score, 0.20),
            (author_fit_score, 0.15),
            (timing_score, 0.10),
            (feasibility_score, 0.10),
        ]),
        components={
            "demand": demand_score,
            "competition": competition_score,
            "monetisation": monetisation_score,
            "author_fit": author_fit_score,
            "timing": timing_score,
            "feasibility": feasibility_score,
        },
        verdict="GO" if total >= 60 else "NO_GO"
    )
```

### The threshold

A score of 60 or above is a GO. Below 60 is a NO_GO. This isn't arbitrary — it's calibrated against historical project outcomes. Books scoring above 60 consistently sell; books below 60 consistently stall.

If the score is below 60, the scorer identifies the weakest components. Maybe demand is high but author fit is low — in which case the project needs a co-author or a different topic. Maybe competition is the problem — in which case the positioning strategist needs a sharper angle.

## Persisting research artifacts

All research artifacts are stored in the database with full provenance:

```python
class ResearchArtifact(Base):
    __tablename__ = "research_artifacts"
    
    id = Column(String, primary_key=True)
    project_id = Column(String, ForeignKey("projects.id"))
    artifact_type = Column(String)  # "competitor", "keyword", "gap", "signal"
    source = Column(str)           # "mock", "google_trends", "reddit_api", etc.
    data = Column(JSON)            # The actual artifact data
    confidence = Column(Float)     # 0.0 to 1.0, how much we trust this
    retrieved_at = Column(DateTime, default=datetime.utcnow)
```

The confidence field matters. Mock data has confidence 0.5. Verified data from authoritative sources has confidence 0.9+. When the Opportunity Scorer encounters conflicting signals, it weights by confidence.

## Handling uncertainty

Research is never complete. The researcher flags uncertainty explicitly:

```python
@dataclass
class ResearchUncertainty:
    field: str
    reason: str
    impact: str  # "low", "medium", "high"
    resolution: str  # How to resolve: "manual_research", "expert_consultation", "pilot_test"
```

High-impact uncertainties trigger a human-in-the-loop gate before the pipeline continues. Better to pause and resolve than to build a book on shaky assumptions.

## Key takeaways

- Research is three-phase: discovery, analysis, scoring.
- Competitor matrix reveals gaps, not just competitors.
- Demand is multi-signal: search, social, sales velocity.
- Opportunity score is weighted, transparent, and threshold-gated.
- Confidence scoring separates verified data from estimates.
- Uncertainty is flagged explicitly, not hidden.
