The AU AI Compliance Playbook: What Australian Small Businesses Need to Know

September 23, 2026

The Australian privacy landscape shifted in late 2024, and most small businesses didn't notice. If you're using AI tools like ChatGPT, Claude, or Copilot in your business — and especially if you're making automated decisions about customers — you need to understand what changed.

What Actually Changed: APP 1.7 and Automated Decisions

The Privacy Act 1988 was amended to include new provisions around automated decision-making. APP 1.7 now requires businesses to disclose when they use systems that make decisions about individuals with legal or significant effects. This isn't just about AI — it's about any system that replaces human judgment.

For a small business, this means:

  • If you use AI to screen job applicants, you need to disclose it
  • If you use automated scoring for credit or insurance, you need consent
  • If customer-facing chatbots make decisions that affect people, you need a privacy policy that covers it

OAIC Enforcement: The Sweeps Are Real

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) has been conducting "sweeps" — targeted investigations into specific industries and practices. In 2024-2025, they focused on AI and automated decision-making. The penalties for non-compliance aren't theoretical: up to $2.5 million for serious breaches, $50 million for corporations.

For small business, the risk isn't the penalty — it's the reputational damage and operational disruption of an OAIC investigation.

The 90-Day Compliance Checklist

We built a practical checklist that any small business can follow:

  1. Days 1-30: Audit your AI tools. What personal data do they touch? Where does it go?
  2. Days 31-60: Update your privacy policy. Add specific AI disclosure clauses.
  3. Days 61-90: Implement internal AI-use policies. Train staff. Document everything.

Who This Affects

If you're an Australian business with an annual turnover of more than $3 million, or if you handle health records, you're covered by the Privacy Act. But even smaller businesses should care — because your clients (bigger businesses) are starting to demand compliance from their suppliers.
